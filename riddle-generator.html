The history of riddles

Riddles are one of humanity's oldest literary forms, appearing in ancient Sumerian texts, Egyptian papyri, and the Bible. The Sphinx's riddle — "What walks on four legs in the morning, two at noon, and three in the evening?" — is perhaps the most famous ancient riddle. Anglo-Saxon riddles in the Exeter Book (10th century) are some of the earliest examples of English-language literature.

Types of riddles

Classic riddles use metaphorical language and wordplay to describe everyday objects or phenomena — they require lateral thinking to solve. Math riddles combine arithmetic or logic with trick questions, often exploiting common assumptions. Word riddles focus on language itself — letters, spelling, etymology, and double meanings. All three types help develop critical thinking and creative problem-solving skills.

Benefits of solving riddles

Riddles engage both hemispheres of the brain, requiring analytical thinking (to work through the logic) and creative thinking (to consider unconventional interpretations). Regular engagement with riddles and puzzles has been associated with improved memory, enhanced problem-solving skills, and better concentration. They are also a great social activity for families, classrooms, and team-building exercises.

Tips for solving riddles

Don't take the language literally — riddles almost always use metaphor or misdirection. Consider multiple meanings of key words. Think about what the answer could be before the question points you there. If you're stuck, try working backwards from possible answers. And remember: the simpler the answer often seems in retrospect, the better the riddle is designed.
